The Arc: One Effort, Three Phases

Leading the organization and assessing the architecture are not two tracks. They are one effort: every conversation feeds the as-is picture, and the as-is picture gives every communication its substance.

PhaseDaysThemeYour job
11-30Listen and DiscoverPresence, alignment, series launch; build the as-is picture through the rounds
231-60Demonstrate and DefineStructures visibly running; future state defined and the gap mapped
361-90Delegate and DriveEarly wins as proof points; transition roadmap in execution
The 90-day test: If you disappeared for two weeks at day 90, would the architecture council still meet, the reviews still happen, and the decisions still get logged?

Critical dependency chain

  1. Leadership alignment (Director/Deputy) before Email 2 goes out
  2. Staff engineers briefed in person before the workforce reads the decision-rights email
  3. Team rounds complete and as-is inventory built before Email 5 goes out
  4. Director signed off on future state and sequencing before anything is announced
  5. Director aligned on the story before the 90-day reflection email

D1Email 1 · Introduction and How We'll Lead
Subject: Introducing Your Technical Director

Team [ORG NAME],

As we bring together the best of [LEGACY ORG 1], [LEGACY ORG 2], and [LEGACY ORG 3] into a single organization, I want to introduce myself and explain how our senior leadership team will work. Some of you I've worked alongside for years. For others, this is our first engagement, and I'm looking forward to changing that quickly.

Who I am. I've spent [X years] building and leading software engineering organizations, most recently as [PRIOR ROLE] for [PRIOR ORG], where I [KEY ACCOMPLISHMENT]. Before that, [1-2 SENTENCE CAREER ARC]. Outside of work, [OPTIONAL PERSONAL NOTE]. I share this not as a resume but so you know the lens I bring: I care deeply about the craft of software engineering, the humans who practice it, and the users who depend on what we build.

How we'll lead. The Director, [DIRECTOR NAME], owns the mission, the resources, and the outcomes of this organization. The Deputy Director, [DEPUTY NAME], owns the operations that keep us running. My job as Technical Director is to translate the mission [DIRECTOR NAME] sets into a technical strategy that achieves it, and to build the engineering health that makes execution possible: the architecture, the standards, and the growth of our technical workforce.

Let me be clear about what that means. Technology is not the point; the mission is. Every architectural decision, every platform investment, and every standard we adopt has to earn its place by moving us toward the objectives the Director has laid out. There will be no tech for tech's sake in this office. If I can't draw a straight line from a technical choice to the mission, we don't make that choice.

Think of it as a three-legged stool. Mission, operations, and technology each need a dedicated senior leader, and none of us succeeds without the other two.

What you can expect from me. I will be visible, accessible, and honest. My calendar has open office hours starting [DATE], and I'll be making rounds to every team over the next [TIMEFRAME]. Bring me your hardest technical problems, your skepticism, and your ideas.

Over the next few weeks I'll share how we'll make technical decisions, the standard we'll hold ourselves to, my expectations of each of us, and where we're headed. Each one will be short. Each one matters.

I'm genuinely excited about what we're going to build together.

Build well,

W2Email 2 · How We'll Make Technical Decisions
Subject: How We'll Make Technical Decisions

Team [ORG NAME],

As promised, here's the second note in this series. This one covers a foundational question for any engineering organization: who makes the technical calls?

One of my first priorities is establishing clear technical decision-making, because ambiguity here is where quality and delivery go to die.

Here's how it will work: our government staff engineers, from individual contributors through senior and principal engineers, together with me, hold the decision authority for the architecture and technical direction of this organization. Staff engineers make the architectural calls, and staff engineers answer for them. That accountability comes with the territory of government service, and we own it fully.

Our contractor teammates are essential to that process. Your expertise, your hands-on knowledge of these systems, and your candid technical input make our decisions better, and I expect staff to actively seek out that input before making calls. But to be clear: informing a decision and making a decision are different things, and the making rests with staff. What that clarity gives all of us, staff and contractor alike, is the ability to move fast once a decision is made, knowing exactly who made it and why.

You'll see this in practice soon: we're standing up [ARCHITECTURE COUNCIL / DECISION RECORD PRACTICE], so decisions and their rationale will be visible to everyone.

Next up in this series: the standard we'll hold ourselves to.

Build well,
[NAME]
Technical Director, [ORG NAME]

What you own outright: [e.g., component-level design, technology selection within approved standards, implementation approach, code review authority]

What comes to the architecture council: [e.g., cross-team interfaces, new platform or service introductions, deviations from standards, anything with acquisition implications]

W3Email 3 · Excellence as Our Operating System
Subject: Excellence as Our Operating System

Team [ORG NAME],

Third note in the series. This one is about the standard we hold ourselves to.

Elizabeth Stone, Netflix's CTO, has described excellence at Netflix not as an aspiration or a poster on the wall, but as an operating system: the way the company actually runs, day in and day out. That is the standard I'm adopting for this office.

Technical excellence will be how we operate, not something we hope emerges. That means we treat software engineering as both an art and a science, and we build with care and intent. Every system we design, every interface we ship, and every line of code we write is a deliberate choice, and I expect us to make those choices thoughtfully rather than by default or convenience.

It also means we are honest about who we build for. It is not us. The most dangerous trap in software engineering is building for ourselves as developers: optimizing for what is elegant to us, familiar to us, or interesting to us, rather than what actually serves the people doing the mission. We will build for our end users, and just as importantly, with them. That means users in the room early and often, requirements grounded in their real workflows, and success measured by whether they can do their jobs better because of what we built, not by whether our architecture diagrams are beautiful.

An operating system is only as good as the processes running on it, so we will back these principles with concrete practice: rigorous design reviews, honest retrospectives, high standards for what we ship, and a culture where the best technical argument wins regardless of who makes it. First up: our engineering tech talk series kicks off [DATE] with [TOPIC/SPEAKER]. More practices follow in the coming weeks, including [ARCHITECTURE REVIEW BOARD / STANDARDS / REVIEW CADENCE].

Next in the series: what I expect of every one of us.

Build well,
[NAME]
Technical Director, [ORG NAME]

W4Email 4 · My Expectations of All of You
Subject: My Expectations of All of You

Team [ORG NAME],

Excellence as an operating system only works if every one of us runs it. Staff or contractor, junior or principal, here is what I expect:

Hone your craft, relentlessly. Software engineering is a discipline that punishes standing still. I expect you to read, experiment, take courses, attend the tech talks, and treat your own growth as part of the job rather than something you do if there's time left over. The engineer you are today should not be the engineer you are a year from now.

Master AI-assisted engineering. AI assistants are now part of the craft, not a shortcut around it. I expect you to learn these tools deeply: when to lean on them, when to distrust them, and how to review their output with the same rigor you'd apply to any teammate's code. Using them well is a skill; using them blindly is a liability. We will invest in helping you build that fluency, starting with [TRAINING/TOOLING PLAN].

Know your users. Deep technical skill aimed at the wrong problem is wasted effort. I expect you to understand the mission your software serves and the people who depend on it, well enough to push back when a requirement doesn't match reality on the ground.

Communicate like it matters, because it does. Write clearly. Document your decisions. Explain your architecture to someone outside your team. The best technical work in the world dies in obscurity if you can't make the case for it.

Disagree openly, commit fully. I expect candor in the room and alignment when we leave it. Challenge ideas hard, including mine. But once a decision is made, we execute as one team.

Lift others as you climb. Mentor someone. Review code generously. Share what you learn. Organizations get strong when knowledge flows freely, and I will notice the people who make others better.

One more note in this series, and it's the big one: where we're headed technically.

Build well,
[NAME]
Technical Director, [ORG NAME]
